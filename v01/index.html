Price Goes Up, Shoppers Pull Back

But not everyone pulls back the same amount, and that difference has a name: elasticity.

Raise the price of something and fewer people buy it. That much nearly everyone knows. The useful question is how many fewer. A bakery that adds 10% to the price of its cakes may see sales drop by 25%. A petrol station that adds 10% may see sales drop by 2%.

Economists call this sensitivity price elasticity of demand. It is simply a measure of how much buyers react when the price moves. Big reaction: demand is elastic. Small reaction: demand is inelastic.

Elastic
Buyers react a lot. Easy to skip, delay or swap: restaurant meals, concert tickets, one brand of cereal.
Inelastic
Buyers barely react. Hard to do without: petrol for a commute, insulin, electricity.

Why the difference?

Three things usually decide it. Are there substitutes? If the shop next door sells the same sandwich, you walk next door. Is it a necessity? You will pay more to heat your home than to see a film. And is it a big bite of your budget? A 10% rise on a car is felt far more than 10% on a pack of gum.

Why it matters

If you sell something elastic, a price rise can backfire: you lose so many customers that total income falls. If you sell something inelastic, a rise can bring in more money, because most customers stay.

For buyers and students the same idea explains why sales promotions exist, why airlines charge more close to departure, and why a tax on cigarettes raises revenue without ending smoking.

The one-minute rule

Before changing a price, ask: if this cost more, what would my customers do instead? If the answer is “go elsewhere,” expect a big reaction. If it is “grumble and pay,” expect a small one.
