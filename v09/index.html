How much less will they buy?

A short essay, about a minute long.

When something costs more, people buy less of it. Most of us feel this without being told. We order the smaller coffee. We wait for the sale.

But there is a quieter question underneath, and it is the one worth asking: how much less?

One

Suppose a bakery and a petrol station both raise prices by a tenth. A week later, the bakery has noticed. The petrol station, mostly, has not.

The bakery’s customers had choices. They could skip the cake, buy a cheaper one, or bake their own. The petrol station’s customers had a commute.

Two

There is a name for this difference: price elasticity. It is a plain idea in a formal coat. It means only how strongly buyers respond when a price moves.

If they respond strongly, we say demand is elastic, like a rubber band that stretches. If they hardly respond, it is inelastic, like a rope that does not.

· · ·

Three

Why do some things stretch and others do not? Usually for three reasons. Whether there is something else to buy. Whether the thing is a need or a wish. And whether it takes a large share of what you earn.

Four

This matters most to whoever sets the price. Raise the price of something elastic, and you may lose so many buyers that you earn less than before. Raise the price of something inelastic, and the money may come in a little faster, because most people simply pay.

The same idea helps the rest of us understand why airlines charge more near the departure date, and why a shop puts the sale sign on the thing we could easily live without.

· · ·

So before you change a price, ask one thing: if this cost more, what would my customers do instead?

The answer will tell you almost everything.
